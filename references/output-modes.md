# Output modes and historical signatures

Choose the deliverable before choosing a period style. Translate named historical references into visual traits; do not ask image generation to copy a specific work.

## Poster

- Default ratio: 2:3 portrait.
- Require one dominant visual, short headline, public-space legibility, and a compact footer only when useful.
- Use the template atlas and campaign recipes.

## Standalone illustration

- Default ratio: 4:5 or match the user's placement.
- Let scene, object, or metaphor lead; text is optional.
- Preserve transport logic through route, rhythm, viewpoint, interchange, vehicle geometry, or destination cues.
- Avoid forcing a poster footer, headline, or border when the user wants artwork rather than advertising.

## Seamless pattern

- Default format: square repeat tile plus one larger preview when supported.
- Build from broad route curves, nodes, seat-like loops, ticket perforations, rails, windows, or simplified landmarks.
- Use 2–5 colours, a clear repeat scale, and edge-to-edge continuity on all four sides.
- Test visually for seams, accidental words, tiny high-frequency stripes, and unintended official symbols.
- Invent new moquette-like rhythms; never reproduce a real TfL seat fabric exactly.

## Fictional logo or transit mark

- Default format: centred 1:1 concept sheet with the main mark, monochrome version, and one small wordmark lockup only if the user asks for a system.
- Use minimal geometry and strong recognition at small size. Consider broken rings, offset ellipses, multiple arcs, rotated or split bars, route knots, or negative-space interchanges.
- Diverge from the official roundel in at least three ways: outer silhouette, central bar orientation or position, proportions, palette, and wording.
- Use a fictional operator name such as `CITY LINES`, `URBAN LINK`, or the user's own name. Do not default to `TRANSPORT FOR LONDON`, `UNDERGROUND`, or a real service name.
- Label presentation sheets `UNOFFICIAL CONCEPT`. Do not imply endorsement.
- For explicit SVG delivery, construct original vector geometry rather than tracing an image-generation result or official asset.

## Decade selector

| Period | Translate into |
|---|---|
| 1920s | decorative modernism, simplified figures, strong framing, warm lithographic paper, early geometric type |
| 1930s | Art Deco speed, Cubist infrastructure, dramatic rays, flat monumental forms, integrated lettering |
| 1940s | reduced wartime palette, civic clarity, economical symbolism, strong silhouettes |
| 1950s | visual wit, optimistic colour, negative-space emblems, sightseeing and leisure |
| 1960s | bolder colour, simplified pop-like form, expressive illustration, photography or collage held by a strict grid |
| 1970s | modular corporate systems, hard-edged geometry, restrained photography, typographic order |
| 1980s–1990s | photographic hero image, strong colour band, metropolitan night lighting, corporate grid |
| 2000s–2010s | commissioned fine art, linocut revival, enamel-like geometry, local landmark reduction |
| 2020s onward | LED-native colour, motion ribbons, deep-screen perspective, generative or data-reactive systems |

Do not age a design only by adding sepia or distress. Change its structure, colour logic, type proportions, and plausible print method.

## Historical designer signals

Use names as routing cues, then remove the name from the final image-generation prompt in favour of these traits:

- Edward McKnight Kauffer: interwar Cubist geometry, energetic diagonals, bold text-image integration, industrial power, opaque lithographic colour.
- Abram Games: one concise visual idea, witty transformation, strong negative space, controlled airbrush or screenprint modelling, minimal explanatory copy.
- Hans Schleger / Zero: rigorous modernist symbol, simplified transport object, crisp sans-serif hierarchy, economical colour, immediate public-information clarity.
- Edward Bawden: bold contour, linocut or block-print character, flattened British narrative scene, decorative but organised detail, earthy colour.
- Horace Taylor: bright decorative colour, theatrical city atmosphere, simplified fashionable figures, rhythmic architecture.
- Tom Purvis: monumental flat silhouettes, almost no outlines, few opaque colours, confident cropping, clear destination block.

Combine a designer signal with a new subject and one template family. Never reproduce a known poster's exact subject, slogan, layout, or mark.
