# Research notes and sources

These notes capture the sources used to build the skill in August 2026. Use them to explain historical grounding, not as assets to copy.

## Primary and museum sources

- [London Transport Museum — Posters](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/stories/design/posters): overview of a collection spanning more than a century and thousands of transport posters.
- [The birth of London's transport posters](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/stories/design/birth-londons-transport-posters): early pictorial publicity and the commissioning culture associated with Frank Pick.
- [The golden age of London's transport posters](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/stories/design/golden-age-londons-transport-posters): 1920s–1930s work and the breadth of artists commissioned for Underground and London Transport publicity.
- [Brightest London: Art Deco in London Transport posters](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/stories/design/brightest-london-art-deco-london-transport-posters): period context and Art Deco's expression of speed, leisure, modern life, and night-time London.
- [London's transport posters from the 1980s onwards](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/stories/design/londons-transport-posters-1980s-onwards): corporate identity, high design standards, cultural commissions, and later poster practice.
- [Poster techniques](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/stories/design/poster-techniques): production methods and varied material approaches.
- [Poster Parade](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/poster-parade): the museum's rotating selection from more than 5,000 posters spanning over 100 years.
- [London by Design — five iconic posters](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/blog/london-design-five-iconic-posters-museums-collection): examples including Man Ray and Alan Rogers that show modernist abstraction and visual economy.
- [Play between 6 and 12, Edward McKnight Kauffer, 1931](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/collections-online/posters/item/1983-4-2999): text integrated with bold geometric forms.
- [Power — the nerve centre of London's Underground, Edward McKnight Kauffer, 1931](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/collections-online/posters/item/1983-4-2996): modernist geometry used to express electrical urban infrastructure.
- [London Transport at London's service, Abram Games, 1947](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/collections-online/posters/item/1983-4-6144): a concise visual joke using a familiar transport emblem.
- [The Tate Gallery by Tube, David Booth, 1987](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/collections-online/posters/item/2007-4241): a source object transforms into a route system; a useful precedent for T13 without copying the real map.
- [Pride 2023: Every Story Matters, Made by TfL](https://madeby.tfl.gov.uk/2023/06/27/pride-2023/): contemporary use of portraits, staff-created artworks, abstract line systems, inclusive figures, and campaign extensions across vehicles and stations.
- [TfL — Our roundel](https://tfl.gov.uk/corporate/about-tfl/culture-and-heritage/our-roundel): history of the bar-and-circle mark, Johnston's commission, the 1938 Standard Signs Manual, and the roundel's continuing trademark role.
- [TfL travel information fascia standard](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/tfl-travel-information-fascia-standard.pdf): documents New Johnston as exclusive to TfL and gives official identity rules. This supports the skill's decision to use generic humanist alternatives rather than claim the real typeface.
- [TfL attractions signage standards](https://foi.tfl.gov.uk/FOI-0092-2526/ATTRACTIONS%20SIGNAGE%20STANDARDS.pdf): describes Johnston 100's legibility and friendly tone and demonstrates restrained high-contrast information design.
- [TfL equality impact assessment for London Overground naming](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/london-overground-naming-eqia.pdf): warns against sensory overload, overuse of colours, and visually uncomfortable stripe patterns; supports the accessibility checks.

## Design conclusions

1. There is no single timeless TfL poster look. The durable system is a commissioning philosophy: make public information and urban invitation visually immediate while allowing artists to work in current visual languages.
2. The most reusable compositional devices are type integrated with image, one strong metaphor, simplified geometry, limited inks, and a small factual service line.
3. Period texture should come from a plausible print process, not a generic vintage overlay.
4. The roundel, Tube map, and controlled typeface are identity assets, not necessary ingredients for an inspired poster.
5. Contemporary work can be photographic, participatory, illustrative, patterned, or typographic while retaining a public-service tone and system-level clarity.
6. The supplied `HIT THE ROAD` image is treated only as a user reference. Its provenance was not verified, so the skill extracts visual evidence without labelling it an official TfL campaign.
7. A user-supplied August 2026 deep-research package proposed a nine-phase model from 1908 pictorial publicity through digital and generative environments. The skill retains the phase model and six-slot prompt anatomy but does not bundle its generated phase images because they contain official-looking marks, real operator wording, and visible generation watermarks.
8. A second user-supplied TfL poster skill contributed phase-specific hex starting points, a slot-level troubleshooting method, and the idea of deterministic SVG starters. Its bundled prompts and example assets were not copied because they defaulted to official wordmarks, roundel constructions, controlled typeface claims, real station or operator wording, and close historical recreations. Five new debranded SVG starters were built from general layout principles instead.
